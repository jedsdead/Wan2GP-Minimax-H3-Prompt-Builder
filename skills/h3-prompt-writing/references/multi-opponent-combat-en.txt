"JACKIE CHAN-STYLE 1-VS-MANY" DIRECTORIAL LOGIC
==================================================

1. The Protagonist is the Absolute Anchor
   - The camera is consistently oriented around the protagonist.
   - The protagonist's body, movement trajectory, active movements, and hit reactions must remain readable at all times.
   - Enemies are not the visual focal point; the protagonist is.
   - The camera tracks the protagonist's movement rather than trying to balance focus equally across all opponents.

2. Multi-Combat is Overlapping Pressure, Not Turn-Based
   - Before one opponent's strike finishes its follow-through, the next opponent is already initiating their attack.
   - As the protagonist dodges one strike, another attacker capitalizes on the opening to step in.
   - As the protagonist drives back an attacker, another opponent immediately forces them to reorient before they can pursue.
   - Never stage combat in clean, isolated waves where Wave 1 fully clears before Wave 2 begins.
   - The core feeling: constant, relentless pressure, while the actual number of opponents directly striking the protagonist at any single split-second is usually 1–2.

3. Active Attackers Are Usually 1–2 at a Time
   - Many opponents can populate the scene, but only 1–2 actively engage in the immediate strike-defense chain at any given second.
   - Other opponents must be seen:
     Closing distance, flanking, cutting off escape routes, waiting for an opening, recovering balance, standing back up, advancing from the background, or emerging from behind obstacles.
   - The essence of 1-vs-many is not "everyone swinging simultaneously," but rather "the protagonist being relentlessly pressured by different angles in continuous relays."

4. Enemies Do Not Need Full Frame Presence
   - The protagonist must remain clearly framed.
   - The immediate active attacker must be clear.
   - The next incoming attacker can enter partially: a stray limb, a shoulder, an arm, a sweeping leg entering the foreground, or half a silhouette closing in from behind an obstacle or the edge of frame.
   - Do not force every opponent to fit cleanly inside the frame at once.
   - Use partial framing—a hand entering from off-screen, a shoulder turning past a pillar, a kick cutting across the foreground—to establish the sense of being swarmed.

5. The Core of 1-vs-Many Is Spatial Blocking
   - The protagonist constantly moves to manage and manipulate the encirclement.
   - Map out the protagonist's 10-second movement pathway first, then direct how opponents intercept and cut into that path.
   - Opponent movements are staged around the protagonist's traversal.
   - The protagonist does not stay planted in one spot parrying endless strikes.
   - The protagonist sidesteps, circles around columns, slips through narrow corridors, pivots along table edges, slips outside obstacles, and constantly resets their defensive angle.

6. The Environment Exists to Funnel and Compress Attack Angles
   - The environment is not the protagonist; avoid turning the fight into a prop showcase.
   - The primary function of scenery is to:
     Restrict pathways, alter approach angles, obstruct opponents, create chokepoints, force enemies into single-file lanes, offer fleeting leverage/bracing points, and cause kinetic collisions.
   - For example:
     A column prevents two enemies from closing in simultaneously;
     A long table forces enemies to run around the perimeter;
     A doorframe narrows an oncoming rush;
     Cargo crates temporarily reduce a 1-vs-4 rush into an isolated 1-vs-1 or 1-vs-2.
   - Environmental interaction should look like bodies moving through space and having their trajectories redirected by architecture, rather than pausing to play with furniture.

7. The Human Body Remains the Focal Point During Environmental Beats
   - Incorporate:
     Vaulting or pushing off a table, brushing past a pillar, slamming into a doorframe, bracing against a wall, getting blocked by a crate, pivoting along a railing, squeezing through a tight opening.
   - Minimize:
     Pausing to hoist chairs, prolonged juggling of props, or destroying set pieces purely for destruction's sake.
   - Reserve heavy prop destruction or environmental finishers for deliberate climax beats.

8. Impacts and Failures Are Essential
   - The protagonist is not an untouchable machine.
   - They can absorb glancing blows, lose footing, get forced back, get grabbed, mistime a move, or endure brief clinches.
   - These setbacks immediately carry kinetic inertia into subsequent actions rather than snapping back instantly to neutral.
   - The same applies to enemies:
     An opponent struck backward can stumble back into the fight seconds later;
     An enemy shoved aside leaves the immediate chain temporarily without being permanently eliminated.

9. Not Every Opponent Needs to Be Knocked Out
   - In realistic multi-combat, many opponents are simply repelled, shoved off balance, forced on a wider flank, or knocked out of the active chain temporarily.
   - Only knock out or permanently drop an opponent when a heavy strike explicitly calls for it.
   - Fallen opponents should become physical obstacles within the space—blocking lanes, forcing others to step around, and redirecting oncoming movement.

10. Incorporate Rhythmic "Breathing Beats"
    - Do not saturate all 10 seconds with unrelenting, unbroken combinations.
    - Include split-second adjustments: a micro-stumble, a quick breath, a frantic scan of the next approaching threat, or a brief re-establishment of distance.
    - These micro-pauses heighten cinematic realism and preserve spatial readability.

11. Camera Language
    - Camera framing prioritizes kinetic readability over dizzying visual noise.
    - Use medium to medium-wide shots during complex multi-opponent blocking to clarify spatial relationships.
    - Punch in tighter for localized impacts, heavy strikes, or immediate reaction beats.
    - When a major repositioning occurs, use fluid camera movement to organically reset spatial orientation.
    - In a continuous take (oner), track the protagonist's kinetic momentum. If cutting is involved, cut on action phrases or major spatial shifts, never on every isolated punch.

12. Strict Cause-and-Effect in Action Beats
    - Every major movement follows:
      Action initiation
      → Trajectory of body shift
      → Trajectory of the strike
      → Hit / Miss / Block / Deflection
      → Immediate physical reaction
      → Displacement / Loss of balance / Recovery
      → Seamless entry into the next action from that exact posture
    - Each beat must organically set up the kinetic premise for the next.
    - Avoid the disjointed pattern of attack → reset → attack → reset.

13. Diegetic Sound Design
    - Synchronize all strikes, swishes, misses, shuffling footwork, rustling cloth, table thuds, structural impacts, wall slams, grunts, and heavy breathing precisely with the visual action.
    - Off-screen audio cues (rapid footsteps approaching, sliding soles, fabric rustle from the flanks) are crucial to sustaining the tension of an active encirclement.
    - Avoid over-the-top, superhero-style sound effects; prioritize grounded, tactile Foley.
