360 PANORAMA LOCATION REFERENCES (Ref2VA)

Use this guide whenever a reference image is a 360-degree (equirectangular) panorama
of a location. It governs how the panorama is defined, how shots are anchored in it
and how spatial consistency is kept across shots and sliding windows. ref-en.txt still
governs labels, section order and retention markers; base-en.txt still governs shots,
camera vocabulary and dialogue.

1. HOW H3 TREATS A PANORAMA

A panorama is a map of a place, not a frame. Left undirected, the model tends to
paste the whole stretched panorama into the video, most often in the first shot and
whenever a shot is only called "wide". Every instruction below exists to make the
model film ordinary perspective views from inside the location, one direction at a
time, while keeping every landmark where the panorama puts it.

2. DEFINE THE PANORAMA WITH THE DEWARP SENTENCE

In subject_definitions, name the image as a 360 panorama, say what it depicts, and
state explicitly that it is dewarped into a flat rectilinear view. This sentence
comes from a community-tested prompt and is the single most important line:

  <Subject 1> is the [location] shown in <Picture 1>, a 360 panorama reference
  depicting the overall look of the location and the position of its key objects.
  For the target video the picture is dewarped and remapped into a flat rectilinear
  lens projection view; the full panorama never appears as a frame.

Then list the landmarks (see section 3). Repeat the dewarp idea in two more places:

- retention_analysis: "<Subject 1> (appears in every shot): fully_preserved - filmed
  as flat rectilinear views dewarped from the 360 panorama in <Picture 1>, each shot
  showing only the part that lies in the direction the camera faces, with every
  landmark in its original position relative to the others."
- The opening of detailed_description, as a camera rule: an ordinary perspective
  cinema camera standing inside the location, a natural field of view of about
  40 degrees, a flat horizon and straight vertical lines; the curved, stretched
  panorama layout is never reproduced.

3. BUILD A DIRECTION MAP FROM THE IMAGE

In an equirectangular image, horizontal position is direction (yaw):

  image center         = straight ahead   (call it north, or 0 degrees)
  halfway to the right = 90 degrees right (east)
  halfway to the left  = 90 degrees left  (west)
  left and right edges = directly behind  (south, 180 degrees; the edges join)
  top / bottom         = straight up / straight down

Before writing any shot, list the landmarks in each direction: the dominant
structure, doors, stairs, railings, edges and drops, props on the floor, the view
beyond each side, and the sun or main light. Write this as one fixed paragraph and
repeat it word for word in every shot list and every sliding window, for example:

  Spatial layout, fixed for every shot: the carved tower is north; the two spires
  with staircases are north-west and north-east; the sun sits low in the
  north-north-west so that anything facing it is backlit, and shadows stretch to the
  south-east; the east railing overlooks the city canyon; the south railing
  overlooks the skyline.

4. ANCHOR EVERY SHOT TWICE

Give each shot both anchors:

- Direction: "the camera faces north-east", "a reverse shot facing south".
- Image region: "the two grey doors on the right side of <Picture 1>", "the gate
  near the right edge of <Picture 1>". Image-region anchors point the model at
  exact pixels of the reference and are very reliable for the first shot.

Describe only what lies in that direction. In a reverse shot, the landmarks behind
the characters must be the ones opposite on the map.

5. MOVE THE CAMERA IN DEGREES

Camera moves that travel around the panorama should state the rotation and what is
revealed: "the camera pans left by 90 degrees from the tower to the west railing",
"the camera pans left by 180 degrees, revealing the far end of the corridor behind".
The direction of the pan must agree with the map (panning left from north reaches
west). Degree-based pans are the cleanest way to show the space in one continuous
shot.

6. NEVER OPEN ON AN UNFRAMED WIDE

Never begin a video or a window with a bare "wide shot" of the location, and never
write "the whole arena" or "the full rooftop". Open with a medium or medium-wide
shot that names the direction, the image region and the edges of the frame, for
example: "A medium-wide, eye-level shot facing due north frames only the lower half
of the tower with its doorway, the base of the north-west spire just entering the
left edge, with no sky above the tower's upper floors." Naming what sits at the
frame edges gives the model a place to stop. A character in the foreground (seen
over the shoulder, or a hand on a prop) makes a pasted panorama even less likely.
If it still happens, crop a normal 16:9 view in the opening direction and add it as
a second picture for the first shot.

7. LIGHT MUST MATCH THE DIRECTION

Fix the sun or key light to a direction on the map. A camera facing the light sees
silhouettes or rim light; a camera with the light behind it sees lit faces. Keep
shadow direction constant. Check every shot against this before finalising.

8. MOVING ELEMENTS AND PEOPLE IN THE PANORAMA

Decide explicitly what happens to everything alive in the image:
- Traffic, crowds, flags, smoke, water: keep them moving at a stated, steady pace in
  every shot ("airspeeders drift slowly and steadily along their paths").
- Background figures: either keep them in their mapped positions and say how they
  behave (react without approaching, intervening or speaking; lips closed), or
  remove them and mark retention partially_preserved with the reason.

9. SLIDING WINDOWS

In Wan2GP sequences, repeat the subject definition, the dewarp sentence, the camera
rule and the spatial layout paragraph in every window, since each window is its own
generation. End each window except the last on a framed wide that names its
direction ("a wide shot facing east of the two fighters at the east railing"), so
the overlap latches onto a known view. Keep the action moving through the map in a
clear route (for example tower, west stairs, plaza, south-east hatches, east
railing) so the space reads as one place.

10. QUALITY CHECK

- Does subject_definitions call it a 360 panorama and include the dewarp sentence?
- Does every window repeat the camera rule and the spatial layout paragraph?
- Is the first shot framed, directional and bounded, with no bare "wide"?
- Does every shot state the direction the camera faces, and only show landmarks
  from that direction?
- Are reverse shots and pans consistent with the map?
- Does lighting match each camera direction?
- Are moving elements and background figures accounted for?
